# Lesson 1 — The rules, and what would have got us disqualified

Six rules with teeth, plus one gate that ends your run even if you place well.

## Rule 1 — The time rule (the big one)

> *"Every engineered feature for a transaction at time t may only use
> information strictly before t."*

**Plain meaning:** when we compute anything about a transaction that happened at
3:00 PM, we may only look at transactions that happened before 3:00 PM. Never
after.

**Why it exists:** in the real world, when a transaction arrives, the future has
not happened yet. A fraud system must decide *now*, with only the past
available. A model that peeks at the future will score brilliantly in the
competition and be worthless in production, because production has no future to
peek at.

**What breaking it looks like:** computing "how many transactions did this
device make in the *next* hour?" That is enormously predictive of fraud — a
compromised device fires off a burst — but it is unknowable at the moment the
transaction arrives.

**We built exactly this and threw it away.** Forward-looking window features
(counts over *[t, t+w]*, time-to-next-transaction) measured **+0.024 local
PR-AUC — our largest single gain of the entire competition**, bigger than
anything we shipped. Unambiguously against this rule, so we deleted them and
submitted nothing containing them, then added a check that crashes the build if
they ever return by accident.

## Rule 2 — You may use test.csv, but never its answers

> *"Feature computation that uses only the raw, non-target columns of test.csv
> in a strictly-past-only way is fine (e.g., a device's known transaction
> history can include test-period rows that occurred earlier than the row being
> scored); using the fraud label anywhere near test.csv is not, since it does
> not exist for you."*

Slowly, because this one confuses people:

- test.csv **has** the transaction columns — amounts, devices, timestamps. It is
  only the `fraud` answer that is missing.
- So when scoring a test transaction on 20 August, we **may** use that device's
  earlier test transactions from 18 August as history. Those are facts about the
  past, and a real system would have them.
- What we may **never** do is use fraud labels anywhere near test — which we
  could not anyway, since we do not have them.

**Why it exists:** it is the honest version of the real world. A real fraud
system absolutely knows what a device did yesterday, including things it has not
labelled yet.

## Rule 3 — No random shuffling in validation

> *"Random K-fold cross-validation is not appropriate for this task... Use
> time-based, walk-forward, expanding-window, or purged/embargoed validation
> instead."*

**Plain meaning:** we need a way to test ourselves before submitting. The
standard method — K-fold — shuffles all rows randomly, hides a fifth, trains on
the rest, and checks. **The organisers banned it.**

**Why:** shuffling destroys time. After a shuffle, training data contains June
transactions while test data contains May transactions, so the future is
predicting the past. The score comes out beautifully high and means nothing.

**What we did instead:** always train on a block of time, then test on the block
immediately after it, then slide forward. No shuffle anywhere in the project.

## Rule 4 — Find patterns, don't memorise identities

> *"Attempting to reverse-engineer or scrape the organizer's private
> fraud-generation logic, or targeting specific entity IDs rather than
> behavioral patterns, undermines the spirit of the competition and may be
> flagged during reproducibility review."*

The data was generated by a program the organisers wrote. Working out *that
program's* logic would win without doing any fraud detection. Banned.

Also banned: memorising that customer `C031724` is a fraudster. That is a
blacklist, not fraud detection, and it does not generalise to a customer never
seen before.

**How we complied, concretely:** the raw ID columns — `customer_id`,
`merchant_id`, `device_id`, `transaction_id` — **never enter the model at all**.
They are used only to *group* transactions so behaviour can be computed ("what
has this device done recently?"). The model never sees which device it is, and
an assertion fails the run if an ID reaches the feature matrix.

**The distinction to be able to state:** grouping by an ID to measure behaviour
is not the same as targeting that ID.

## Rule 5 — No external data

> *"No external data of any kind."*

No holiday calendars, no fraud datasets from elsewhere, no pretrained models fed
on other data. Only the two CSVs. We used nothing else.

## Rule 6 — Five submissions per day

A hard cap; exceed it and submissions are rejected. One subtlety that cost us
time: the day resets at **00:00 UTC = 06:00 Dhaka**, not local midnight. A
submission made at 14:07 Dhaka was logged 08:07 UTC and counted against a
different day than assumed.

## The gate that is not a rule but ends your run anyway

> *"Top 15 private leaderboard teams must submit a notebook and short summary
> for reproducibility verification to qualify for the onsite final."*

You can finish 2nd and still be eliminated if the notebook does not reproduce
the score, or if a reviewer finds something in it that breaks the rules above.
**This is why we wrote a methodology document that openly discloses our one
judgement call** rather than staying quiet and hoping.

# Lesson 2 — Why there are two leaderboards

## The mechanic

The 262,648 test rows are secretly split:

| | Share | When you see it |
|---|---|---|
| **Public** leaderboard | 60% of rows | Live, during the contest, every submission |
| **Private** leaderboard | 40% of rows | Only after the deadline. The real result. |

You do not know which rows are which. You submit predictions for all 262,648;
the organisers score 60% and show you that number. The other 40% are scored
silently and revealed at the end.

## The problem it solves: you can cheat without meaning to

Imagine you have no idea what you are doing and submit 100 random guesses. By
luck, one scores higher than the others. You conclude "idea #57 works!"

It did not work. **You found the luckiest of 100 random numbers.**

The realistic version: you have five decent ideas, submit all five, and two
score higher. You keep those and build on them. This *feels* like careful
experimentation — but if the differences between the ideas are smaller than the
random variation in scoring, you are still selecting noise, and the next round of
work is built on a conclusion that was never true.

This is **overfitting the leaderboard**, and it gets worse the more you submit.
The leaderboard slowly stops measuring your model and starts measuring your luck.

## How the private split kills it

Luck on the public 60% does not carry over to the private 40% — different rows.

A team that genuinely improved rises on both. A team that got lucky rises on the
public board and then **falls on the private one**. That collapse has a name —
the *shakeup* — and it routinely throws teams from the top ten into the middle.

The private leaderboard is an exam the students cannot see the answers to.

## Why the 5-per-day limit exists too

Same reason: it is a budget on how many times you may consult a noisy signal.
Ten submissions across the whole contest means you cannot probe your way to a
good score. You have to be right *before* you submit.

## What this meant for us specifically

1. **We had to build our own scoreboard.** With ~10 submissions the leaderboard
   could not be a testing tool. We built local time-based validation and used
   the leaderboard only to *confirm* things we already believed.
2. **Our brief moment at #1 was meaningless.** We led with 0.56492, ahead of
   second by **0.00013** — a rounding error on 60% of the data. Our own status
   notes read: *"Do not treat this as safe."* We finished 2nd on the public board.
3. **We got to select two submissions for private scoring.** If you nominate
   nothing, it defaults to your best two *public* scores — which would have been
   our two riskiest. So we chose deliberately: the champion at 0.56548, plus a
   conservative pipeline at 0.55368 containing no judgement calls at all. If a
   reviewer disagreed with our reading of Rule 1, we still had something clean
   on the board. That pairing is a hedge, and judges tend to respect it.

# Lesson 3 — The compliance checks, and where each one lives

**Everything about this project is answered from the champion notebook**
(`~/Downloads/react-2026-overfit-overcaffeinated-champion.ipynb`), because that
is the submitted entry. The repo's `STATUS.md`, `METHOD_SUMMARY.md` and
`data/processed/*.csv` describe **arm C / v8c / v9c** — a different, compliant
pipeline. Mixing them produces wrong answers.

## What runs inside the champion notebook

| Check | What it does | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| Forward-name guard | Matches every feature name against a list of forward-looking markers, and refuses to run on a match | Features that read the future, by *name* |
| First-transaction check | A customer's first-ever row must show **blank** prior statistics, and must register as a new-device pairing | Prior statistics that leaked backwards in time |
| ID and label ban | Asserts the four raw ID columns — customer, merchant, device, transaction — and the fraud label are absent from the matrix, on **every call** | Entity targeting (Rule 4), or the answer reaching the model |

All three are **called**, not merely defined. The compliance claim therefore
holds in the artifact the judges are handed.

## What lives only in the repository

| Check | What it does |
|---|---|
| `scripts/test_compliance.py` | Perturbs a **later** row, recomputes, and requires that **no earlier row's features change** — pastness verified empirically rather than by naming convention |

This is the strongest of the four, because it reads no names at all. But it is
not in the notebook, so do not cite it as if a judge could open the notebook and
find it.

## How to answer "how do you know there is no leakage?"

> Three assertions run inside the submitted notebook — forward-name checking,
> first-transaction blank verification, and a raw-ID and label ban enforced on
> every call. A fourth, empirical test in our repository perturbs a later row
> and requires that no earlier row's features move.

**The reserve line, if pressed hard:** the team that measured the largest
forward-looking gain of anyone and deleted it is not the team trying to get
something past you.

## A wording risk in the champion's own header

The notebook describes its neural network as using **"categorical entity
embeddings"**. That phrase invites exactly the wrong question — *did you embed
`customer_id`?*

It does not. What it embeds is five categorical **attributes** — merchant
category, device type, location, payment method and transaction type — plus
three combinations of them (payment method crossed with device type, category
crossed with location, transaction type crossed with payment method).

**No entity identifiers anywhere.** The header's wording is loose; the pipeline
is clean. Pre-empt it:

> Our neural network embeds transaction attributes — merchant category, device
> type, location, payment method — not entity identifiers. Raw IDs are banned by
> assertion before the model sees anything.

# Lesson 5 — What "signal" means

## The definition

**Signal is anything that makes fraud look different from legitimate.**

That's it. If you can point at a column and say "fraud tends to have higher values here" or "fraud clusters in this category" — that column carries signal.

The opposite is **noise**: variation that has nothing to do with fraud. Random wobble. It moves the numbers around but tells you nothing about who's a fraudster.

Every column in your dataset is some mix of the two. The entire job of feature engineering is to build columns that are mostly signal.

## Why it's always relative

A column doesn't "have signal" on its own. It has signal **about a specific question**.

`account_age_days` carries plenty of information — it tells you exactly how long someone has banked with you. That's useful for a marketing team. But for the question *"is this fraud?"* it carries **nothing**, because fraudsters and legitimate customers have identical median account ages of 350 days.

So the phrase always means: *signal about fraud*. Information about anything else is, for us, noise.

## How you measure it

Two ways, both of which appear in your project.

**1. Lift** — the crude, readable one.

> Lift = (fraud rate in this group) / (fraud rate overall)

Base rate is 1.76%. If `ussd` transactions are 3.21% fraud, lift = 3.21 / 1.76 = **1.83x**. Fraud is 1.83 times more concentrated there than average.

- Lift of **1.0** = no signal. This group is exactly as fraudulent as everything else.
- Lift **above 1** = risk concentrates here.
- Lift **below 1** = this group is safer than average.

**2. Univariate AP** — the precise one your team used.

"Univariate" means *one variable at a time*. You take a single column, use it alone as your fraud score, rank all rows by it, and measure PR-AUC. It answers: *if this one column were my entire model, how good would it be?*

That's where numbers like `cust_amt_robust_z: 0.41` come from in your documents — that one feature, alone, scores 0.41. Compare with the base rate of 0.0176 for random ordering, and you can see it's carrying a great deal.

## Now, every line where I used the word

### "Amount — a strong signal"

> Legitimate median **486 tk**, fraud median **2,311 tk**.

Fraud is 5x larger at the median. If you knew nothing else and just ranked every transaction by amount, you'd do considerably better than random. **Strong** here means the two groups are far apart.

### "Hour of day — a very strong signal"

> 3 AM: **17.25%** fraud, lift **9.8x**. Working hours: ~1.2%, lift ~0.7x.

Nearly one in six transactions at 3 AM is fraud, against one in eighty during the day. The separation is enormous — hence "very strong".

But hold that thought. I'll come back to it, because this line was slightly misleading and it's the most instructive thing in this lesson.

### "The categorical columns — weak signals"

> ussd 1.83x, travel 1.99x, topup 1.46x, credit_card 1.29x.

**Weak** means the groups differ, but only a little. Travel merchants are twice as risky as average — real, worth having, but it doesn't come close to identifying fraud. 96.5% of travel transactions are still perfectly legitimate. A model built on this alone would be nearly useless.

### "`account_age_days` — no signal at all"

> Median 350 days for fraud. Median 350 days for legitimate.

The distributions sit on top of each other. Knowing the account age moves your belief about fraud **by nothing**. Lift ~ 1.0 everywhere. This is what a dead column looks like when you actually check.

### "Over two-thirds of the achievable signal is not in the row at all"

The raw-columns model scored **0.16644**. The finished model scored **0.56548**.

So roughly two-thirds of what could be learned was invisible to a model that only ever looked at one transaction at a time. It only appears when you compare a transaction to its own history.

### "Every single-column signal I just showed you misses it completely"

Device D000775: twelve frauds in seven minutes at **340–780 taka**, at **10:45 PM**, on a `mobile_app`, buying from restaurants.

Check it against each signal:
- Amount signal? **Misses.** 598 tk is barely above the legitimate median of 486. Utterly ordinary.
- Hour signal? **Misses.** 10:45 PM has a lift of about 1.0. Not a risky hour.
- Device type? **Misses.** `mobile_app` is the *safest* device type, lift 0.82.
- Merchant category? **Misses.** Restaurants are unremarkable.

Every strong signal in the dataset points at "legitimate" for all twelve of these rows. They are only detectable as a **pattern across rows** — twelve in seven minutes on one device.

### The regime-change lines: "every signal we relied on decayed"

This is from your July diagnostic, and now you can read it properly:

| Feature | Univariate AP before July | After |
|---|---|---|
| `cust_amt_robust_z` (is this amount odd for this customer?) | **0.41** | **0.24** |
| `dev_amtsum_6h` (how much has this device pushed in 6 hours?) | **0.095** | **0.172** |

The first feature lost 40% of its power. The second nearly doubled. Same features, same code — the *fraud changed*, so what separated fraud from legitimate changed with it.

"Customer-relative signal halved, device-volume signal doubled" means exactly this table.

### "The decayed signal is still the strongest thing we have"

The obvious response to that table is: throw away the fading feature, keep the rising one. Your team tested it, and it **lost 0.0010**.

The reason is now readable directly from the numbers: **0.24 is still much bigger than 0.172.** The customer feature got worse and *remained the best thing in the set*. There was nothing stronger hiding behind it.

### "There is no magic signal in this dataset"

All 33 teams finished between 0.497 and 0.565. If some column or combination carried a lot more separating power, somebody among 33 teams would have found it and pulled away. Nobody did — so the ceiling is a property of the data, not of anyone's effort.

### "Pseudo-labels inject more noise than signal"

Self-training means: let the model guess labels for unlabelled rows, then train on its own guesses. Your model is right about roughly half of what it flags, so those invented labels are about half wrong — they add more randomness than information. Measured at **-0.0101**. Rejected.

## The thing I glossed over, and it matters

I called 3 AM a "very strong signal", and by lift it is. But look at the volume column:

| Hour | Fraud rate | Lift | Transactions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 AM | 17.25% | 9.8x | **4,291** |
| 12 noon | 1.19% | 0.67x | **49,910** |

Only 4,291 transactions happen at 3 AM in 195 days — under 0.6% of the data. So catching *all* of them catches about 740 frauds out of 12,886. **Roughly 6% of the fraud.**

The other 94% happens at ordinary hours, in ordinary amounts, and the hour column says nothing about it.

**So signal has two dimensions, and lift only shows you one:**

- **Strength** — how different the groups are
- **Coverage** — how many rows it applies to

A signal can be enormously strong and nearly worthless because it's rare. It can be mild and valuable because it touches everything.

This is why the raw-column model stopped at 0.166. Its signals were either strong-and-rare (3 AM) or common-and-weak (merchant category). Nothing was both.

**Feature engineering is the work of building signals that are strong *and* apply to most rows.** `cust_amt_robust_z` at 0.41 univariate AP is exactly that — it asks "is this amount strange *for this particular customer*", and that question can be asked of every single row in the file.

That's Lesson 6.

# Lesson 6 — How 13 columns became 160 features

Source: the feature list and feature functions in the champion notebook.

## The one idea

> Stop asking *"is this transaction unusual?"* and start asking *"is this transaction unusual **for this customer / this device / this merchant**?"*

Everything below is variations on that question.

## Building one feature by hand

Customer **C032720**, their last 8 transactions:

| Date | Amount | Category |
|---|---|---|
| 10 Mar | 59.41 tk | education |
| 11 Mar | 65.01 tk | electronics |
| 11 Mar | 169.24 tk | electronics |
| 12 Mar | 63.55 tk | pharmacy |
| 15 Mar | 75.28 tk | restaurant |
| 15 Mar | 66.37 tk | pharmacy |
| 19 Mar | 160.19 tk | education |
| 20 Mar | 153.93 tk | restaurant |

Then at **01:46 on 21 March**: **6,550.13 tk** — fraud.

Globally that is the ~98th percentile: about 14,600 legitimate transactions are bigger. Useless as a flag. But relative to this customer:

```
prior mean   = 101.62 tk
prior median =  70.83 tk
prior MAD    =   9.35 tk
```

```
cust_amt_ratio     = 6550.13 / 101.62            = 64.5
cust_amt_robust_z  = (6550.13 - 70.83) / 9.35    = 693
```

Both are in the champion's 160. **Same data, better question.** (Why median/MAD instead of mean/standard deviation is in the MAD sidebar — one legitimate fridge purchase takes a standard z-score from 138.9 to 0.16 while the robust version survives.)

## Reading any feature name

```
        dev_amt_vs_recent_max_5m
        |-| |--------------| |-|
         |          |          |
         |          |          +-- window
         |          +------------- what is measured
         +------------------------ entity it is compared against
```

**Entity prefix:** `cust_` `dev_` `merch_` `loc_`

**Measured:** `amt` (amount) · `cnt` (count) · `amtsum` (total money) · `history_count` (lifetime) · `seconds_since_last` (gap) · `fresh_X` (never-seen-before counterparties) · `nunique_X` (distinct counterparties)

**Statistic or window:** `_robust_z` (median-MAD score) · `_zscore` (mean-standard-deviation) · `_ratio` (divided by prior mean) · `_share` (divided by window total) · `_burst_*_ratio` (short over long) · `_velocity_ratio` (actual over expected) · `_gap_accel` (speeding up?) · `_prior` (strictly before) · windows `5m 15m 30m 1h 3h 6h 12h 24h 72h 168h`

Decode three:

- `dev_fresh_customer_id_1h` -> *brand-new customers on this device in the last hour*
- `merch_burst_cnt_ratio` -> *this merchant's 1-hour count divided by its 1-week count*
- `cd_device_id_component_size_prior` -> *how big is the customer-device network this device sits in*

## How many features, exactly

Two numbers circulate in the project and they describe two different pipelines:

## The nine families

**1. Expanding statistics** — everything the entity has ever done.
`cust_amt_mean_prior` `dev_amt_std_prior` `merch_amt_robust_z` `cust_history_count` `dev_amt_sum_prior`

**2. Trailing windows** — 1/6/24/72/168h counts and sums, plus shares and burst ratios.
`dev_amtsum_6h` `merch_cnt_24h` `cust_amt_share_1h` `dev_burst_cnt_ratio`

**3. Sub-hour windows** — down to 5 minutes.
`dev_cnt_5m` `merch_amtsum_30m` `dev_amt_vs_recent_max_5m` `cust_amt_vs_recent_mean_12h`

**4. Novelty** — first time seeing this pairing?
`is_new_device_for_customer` `is_new_customer_for_device` `is_new_merchant_for_customer` `is_new_location_for_customer`

**5. Fan-out** — how many distinct counterparties.
`device_id_nunique_customer_for_device_prior` `customer_id_nunique_merchant_for_customer_share_prior`

**6. Freshness** — how many counterparties in this window are *brand new*.
`dev_fresh_customer_id_1h` `cust_fresh_device_id_share_168h`

`dev_fresh_customer_id_1h` deserves a pause: *how many customers has this device seen for the first time in the last hour*. That is precisely the signature of a compromised device draining multiple accounts, and no raw column can express it.

**7. Self-relative velocity** — is this entity accelerating?

Two questions, both asked against the entity's *own* long-run habit rather than against any global threshold.

*Are the gaps shrinking?* Work out the average gap between this entity's transactions across its whole life, then compare it to the gap since its last one. If an entity normally waits four hours and has just transacted twice in ninety seconds, that ratio is enormous. It says **this entity is behaving faster than it has ever behaved**, which is the shape of an account being drained.

*Is the volume above what this entity would be expected to produce?* From its lifetime rate you can work out roughly how many transactions it *should* make in an hour. Compare that to how many it actually made. Ten times expected is a very different statement from "ten transactions this hour" — a busy merchant makes ten an hour normally, a dormant customer never does. **The comparison is always against the entity's own baseline, never a fixed number.**

A third feature in this family asks whether this is the largest amount the entity has ever sent — a personal record, which for a legitimate customer happens rarely and for a drained account happens on the way out.

**8. Graph features — measuring the network around a transaction**

Every transaction is a link between a customer and a device. Draw that link. Do it for every transaction and you get a web — customers connected to the devices they use, devices connected to the customers who use them.

That web naturally breaks into **islands**. A normal person is a small island: one customer, maybe two or three devices, nobody else attached. But if one device is shared by forty customers, and those customers each use other devices, the islands merge into one large landmass.

**The feature is simply: how big is the island this transaction is standing on?**

A small island means an ordinary, self-contained person. A large one means this transaction sits inside a sprawling network of shared devices and shared customers — which is what an organised fraud ring physically looks like in data.

**The part that makes it legal.** The islands grow over time as more links get drawn. So the question has to be asked in the right order: *measure the island first, then draw this transaction's link.* Draw the link first and a transaction would be inflating the very island it is being judged against — using the present to explain the present. That ordering is the entire compliance guarantee for these features. It is not checked by a separate test; it is built into the order the two steps happen.

Four of the champion's 160 features measure island size — two for the customer-device web, two for the customer-merchant web.

**9. Calendar features, and why a clock is a circle**

Hand a model the hour as a plain number and 23 and 0 are the furthest apart values it can see — 23 units of distance. But 11 PM and midnight are one hour apart. The model has been told the exact opposite of the truth about the one boundary that matters most, because that is precisely where the fraud lives.

The fix is to describe time as a *position on a clock face* rather than a number on a line: two coordinates giving position around the dial. Now 11 PM and midnight sit next to each other, and midnight and noon are genuinely far apart. The geometry finally matches reality. The same treatment is applied to day of week, so Sunday and Monday are neighbours rather than opposite ends of a scale.

Also in this family: how this amount compares to the typical amount in its merchant category, how much money has moved through this location recently, how common this location is as a share of all traffic, and whether this customer *normally* transacts at this hour — that last one personal rather than global. 3 AM is suspicious in general; 3 AM is far more suspicious for someone who has never once transacted at night.

## The design decision worth presenting: windows matched to rhythm

The champion does not use one set of time windows for everything. Each entity gets its own ladder:

| Entity | Shortest window |
|---|---|
| customer | 30 minutes |
| **device** | **5 minutes** |
| merchant | 5 minutes |

A time window only tells you something if the entity actually does things at that rhythm. From Lesson 4:

| Entity | Median transactions each |
|---|---|
| customer | 10 |
| **device** | **28** |
| merchant | 10 |

Devices are nearly three times busier than customers.

Think of it as measuring traffic. On a motorway you can count cars per minute and get a meaningful number. On a village lane, counting per minute gives you zero, zero, zero, zero — technically accurate, completely uninformative. You would need to count per day.

A five-minute *customer* window is a village lane: empty almost always, so the column is nearly all zeros. That is worse than useless — a column that teaches nothing while still competing for the model's attention, since the model only looks at a random subset of features when building each tree. Dead columns crowd out live ones.

A five-minute *device* window is a motorway. Things actually happen in it.

**And this is exactly what catches the headline example.** Device D000775 fired twelve transactions in seven minutes. The device-level five-minute window sees that burst clearly. No customer-level window would have — the shortest there is thirty minutes, and by then the burst is over and diluted.

Good answer to "how did you choose your time windows?" — *we matched them to how often each entity actually transacts, and we measured that first.*

## How "only the past" is actually guaranteed

Three separate ideas, each closing a different door.

**1. The window ends just before now, not around now.** A "last hour" window could mean the hour *around* this transaction or the hour *before* it. Only the second is legal. The champion's windows stop the instant before the current transaction, so a transaction is never counted in its own window.

The distinction sounds pedantic and is not. Include the current row and every window feature quietly contains a trace of the answer, the validation score climbs, and the improvement is fictional. It is the easiest leak in the project to introduce by accident, and it turns on a single choice at the moment the window is defined.

**2. Running totals subtract your own contribution.** For "what has this customer spent historically", the pipeline keeps a running total as it walks forward through time, then subtracts the current transaction's own amount back out before using it. A transaction is compared against its customer's history *excluding itself*.

**3. The graph measures before it connects.** As above.

**The check that proves all three worked.** If the past-only rule holds, a customer's very first transaction has nothing before it — so every historical feature must come back **blank**. Not zero. Blank, as in *undefined, no data exists*.

The notebook checks precisely that: for every customer's first-ever transaction, the historical averages must be blank and the "is this a new device for this customer?" flag must say yes. If a first transaction shows a real number where a blank belongs, information has flowed backwards in time and the run stops.

That is a clever test because it does not inspect the logic — it inspects a *consequence* the logic must produce.

**Two more gates before the model sees anything.**

- **No forward-looking columns.** The pipeline holds a list of naming patterns that mark a feature as reading the future, and refuses to run if any feature matches one. Four such patterns are listed — a fossil record of the features the team built and deleted.
- **No identities, no answers.** The four ID columns — customer, merchant, device, transaction — and the fraud label itself are checked for absence every single time a data matrix is handed to the model. Rule 4 enforced mechanically rather than promised.

**The one-sentence version for a judge:** *compliance is not something we reviewed for, it is something the pipeline refuses to run without.*

# Sidebar to Lesson 6 — What "98th percentile" means

## The idea

Line up all 731,942 transaction amounts from smallest to largest. Then walk along the line and ask: *what value am I standing on when I've passed 98% of them?*

That value is the **98th percentile**.

It means: **98% of transactions are smaller than this, 2% are bigger.**

## On your actual data

Here are the real percentiles of `amount_bdt`:

| Percentile | Amount | Read it as |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 40.64 tk | only 1% of transactions are smaller than 41 taka |
| 25th | 240.25 tk | a quarter of all transactions are under 240 taka |
| **50th** | **492.38 tk** | half are below, half above — **this is the median** |
| 75th | 1,019.20 tk | three-quarters are under ~1,000 taka |
| 95th | 3,031.24 tk | only 5% exceed ~3,000 taka |
| 99th | 7,810.60 tk | only 1% exceed ~7,800 taka |
| 100th | 495,000 tk | the largest transaction in the file |

So the **median you've been seeing everywhere is just the 50th percentile.** Same idea, common enough to have its own name.

## Why I used it for the 6,550 taka fraud

That fraudulent transaction was 6,550 taka. Looking at the table: it's above the 95th (3,031) and below the 99th (7,810). So roughly the **98th percentile** — about 2% of transactions are larger.

Then the arithmetic I did:

```
2% of 731,942 transactions ~= 14,600 transactions are bigger than 6,550 tk
```

And nearly all of those 14,600 are perfectly legitimate — rich customers, business payments, big purchases. So "flag everything above 6,550 taka" would hand the fraud team ~14,600 alerts to catch one fraud we already know about.

**That was the point:** by the standards of the whole dataset, 6,550 taka is unremarkable enough to be useless as a flag. By the standards of *that particular customer* — who never spent more than 169 taka — it was 64x abnormal.

## Why percentiles instead of averages

Because the average is a liar when the data is lopsided.

The **mean** transaction amount is **1,028 taka**. The **median** is **492 taka**. Those are wildly different numbers describing the same column.

Why? Because a handful of enormous transactions — one is 495,000 taka — drag the average upward. More than 70% of your transactions are *below* the "average" transaction. So the mean describes almost nobody.

The median doesn't have that problem. It's a position in the line, not a calculation over values, so one 495,000-taka monster shifts it by essentially nothing.

**This is exactly why your features use `robust_z` (median and MAD) rather than a standard z-score (mean and standard deviation).** Same reasoning, applied per customer instead of across the whole dataset.

## The one-line version

> A percentile is a **rank position expressed as a percentage** — the 98th percentile is the value that 98% of your data falls below.

And since your competition metric is itself a ranking metric, this way of thinking is worth getting comfortable with. Percentiles, medians, ranks — you'll notice the whole project keeps reaching for position rather than magnitude.

# Sidebar to Lesson 6 — MAD (Median Absolute Deviation)

## The one-liner

**MAD is the typical distance from the middle.**

The median tells you where the centre is. MAD tells you how spread out things usually are around that centre.

## Computing it by hand, on your customer

Customer C032720's 8 transactions, sorted:

```
59.41   63.55   65.01   66.37   75.28   153.93   160.19   169.24
```

**Step 1 — find the median.** Eight values, so the middle two are 66.37 and 75.28:
```
median = (66.37 + 75.28) / 2 = 70.83
```

**Step 2 — how far is each value from that median?** (Absolute = ignore the minus sign, we only care about distance.)

| Amount | Distance from 70.83 |
|---|---|
| 66.37 | 4.45 |
| 75.28 | 4.45 |
| 65.01 | 5.81 |
| 63.55 | 7.28 |
| 59.41 | 11.42 |
| 153.93 | 83.10 |
| 160.19 | 89.36 |
| 169.24 | 98.42 |

**Step 3 — take the median of *those* distances.** Middle two are 7.28 and 11.42:
```
MAD = (7.28 + 11.42) / 2 = 9.35
```

So: *this customer's transactions typically sit about **9 taka** away from their usual **71 taka**.*

That's the whole calculation. Median, then distances, then median again.

## What it's for

Once you know the centre (70.83) and the typical wobble (9.35), you can score any new transaction:

```
robust_z = (this amount - median) / MAD
```

For the fraudulent 6,550 taka transaction:
```
(6550.13 - 70.83) / 9.35 = 693
```

**693 typical-wobbles away from normal.** That's the number that makes the fraud unmissable.

## Now — why not just use standard deviation?

Standard deviation is the more familiar version of the same idea, built on mean and squared distances. On this customer it gives:

```
mean = 101.62,  standard deviation = 46.43
plain z = (6550.13 - 101.62) / 46.43 = 138.9
```

That also flags the fraud. So why bother with MAD?

**Because of what happens when one legitimate large purchase appears.**

Let me add a single 40,000 taka transaction to this customer's history — they bought a fridge. Completely legitimate. Watch what it does:

| Statistic | Before | After one fridge | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| median | 70.83 | 75.28 | +6% |
| **MAD** | **9.35** | **15.87** | **+70%** |
| mean | 101.62 | **4,534.78** | **+4,362%** |
| **standard deviation** | **46.43** | **12,538.93** | **+26,900%** |

The mean and standard deviation are **destroyed** by one value. The median and MAD barely notice.

Now score the same fraud again with that fridge in the history:

| | Before the fridge | After the fridge |
|---|---|---|
| plain z (mean/std) | 138.9 | **0.16** |
| **robust_z (median/MAD)** | **693** | **408** |

Read that carefully.

**The standard z-score goes from 138.9 to 0.16.** The fraud becomes completely invisible — 0.16 means "utterly unremarkable, well within normal range". One legitimate fridge purchase blinded the detector.

**The robust version goes from 693 to 408.** Still screaming. Still catches it.

## Why this matters for your project

Real people buy fridges. Real customers have one unusual month, one large medical bill, one wedding. If your fraud detector goes blind every time a customer does something legitimately expensive, it's useless.

`cust_amt_robust_z` scores **0.415 univariate AP** — one of the two strongest features in your entire set. It would not have if it had been built on mean and standard deviation, because thousands of your 38,689 customers have a fridge in their history somewhere.

**That's why "robust" is in the name, and it's a good answer if a judge asks about your feature design choices:** we used median and MAD rather than mean and standard deviation because a single legitimate outlier in a customer's history destroys a mean-based z-score, and we can demonstrate the failure.

## The vocabulary, tidied up

| Term | Plain meaning | Robust to outliers? |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | The average | No |
| Median | The middle value | Yes |
| Standard deviation | Typical spread around the mean | No |
| **MAD** | Typical spread around the median | **Yes** |
| z-score | How many "typical spreads" from the centre | depends which pair you use |

`robust_z` = the median-and-MAD version of a z-score.

# Lesson 7 — The metric: how your score is actually calculated

You have seen the number 0.56548 many times. Here is what produces it, and why four of the team's decisions follow directly from it.

## What you hand in

Not decisions. **Scores.**

For each of the 262,648 test transactions you submit a number between 0 and 1 — how suspicious you think it is. You never say "this one is fraud." You say "this one is more suspicious than that one."

The organisers then sort all 262,648 rows by your number, highest first, and ask a single question:

> **How close to the top did the real frauds end up?**

That is the entire measurement. Nothing else about your submission is examined.

## Watching it work on ten transactions

Suppose there were only 10 transactions and 3 of them were fraud. Two teams submit different scores, so the rows sort differently.

**Team A's ordering** — frauds land at positions 1, 2 and 5:

| Position | Is it fraud? | Frauds found so far | Of how many checked | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **yes** | 1 | 1 | **1.00** |
| 2 | **yes** | 2 | 2 | **1.00** |
| 3 | no | | | |
| 4 | no | | | |
| 5 | **yes** | 3 | 5 | **0.60** |

Take the precision at each moment a fraud was found, and average them:
**(1.00 + 1.00 + 0.60) / 3 = 0.87**

**Team B's ordering** — same three frauds, but at positions 6, 8 and 10:

| Position | Is it fraud? | Frauds found so far | Of how many checked | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | **yes** | 1 | 6 | 0.17 |
| 8 | **yes** | 2 | 8 | 0.25 |
| 10 | **yes** | 3 | 10 | 0.30 |

Average: **0.24**

**Identical data. Identical frauds. Only the order changed — 0.87 against 0.24.**

That is the metric. It is called average precision, or PR-AUC.

## The two words underneath it

**Precision** — of the transactions you flagged, what fraction were actually fraud? Flag 100 and 30 are real: precision 0.30.

**Recall** — of all the fraud that exists, what fraction did you catch? Catch 30 of 100 frauds: recall 0.30.

These trade against each other. Flag only your single most suspicious transaction and precision might be perfect — but you have caught one fraud out of 4,600, so recall is nearly zero. Flag everything and recall is perfect while precision collapses to the base rate.

Average precision walks the entire trade-off curve, from "flag almost nothing" to "flag everything", and summarises how well you held precision up as recall climbed. **A high score means you kept finding real fraud without accumulating false alarms along the way.**

## Why not accuracy

98.24% of your rows are legitimate.

Predict "not fraud" for every single transaction and you are **98.24% accurate**. You would beat almost any real model on that measure, and you would have built nothing.

Accuracy rewards ignoring rare events. That is why nobody scores fraud detection with it.

## What counts as a good score

You need a floor to compare against, and the floor is the fraud rate itself.

**Shuffle the rows into random order and you score about 0.0176** — the 1.76% base rate. That is what "no skill" looks like.

| | Score |
|---|---|
| Random ordering | 0.0176 |
| One hand-built feature alone | ~0.41 |
| Raw columns only | 0.16644 |
| **The champion** | **0.56548** |

So 0.565 is roughly **32 times better than guessing.**

This matters for the presentation. To someone who has not seen a rare-event problem, "0.56" sounds like a coin flip. It is not — on a metric where nothing scores 0.0176, it is a strong result, and the whole leaderboard bunching between 0.50 and 0.565 confirms nobody found much more.

## The consequence that shaped the entire project

### Think of it as a to-do list

You are not catching fraud. You are writing a **to-do list for the fraud team.**

They come in each morning and work down your list from the top. Whatever they do not reach by home time, they do not check.

Your job is to decide **who goes first**.

The scores you submit — 0.88, 0.03, 0.41 — are just a way of putting the list in order. Once the list is sorted, **the scores are thrown away.** Nobody looks at them again.

### Why the numbers themselves do not matter

Imagine a teacher ranks a class by exam marks, then doubles everyone's mark.

Does the class ranking change? No. The top student is still top. Nobody moves.

Same here. Double all 262,648 of your scores, halve them, add 5 to every one — **the list comes out in exactly the same order every time.** And the order is all you are graded on.

This sounds like a technicality. It is not. Four decisions came out of it.

### 1. We skipped calibration

Calibration means adjusting your scores so they are *honest numbers* — so that when you say 0.70, roughly 70% of those really are fraud.

Genuinely useful in a bank. Completely useless here, because nobody reads your numbers. Only the order.

Worse than useless, in fact. Calibration tends to squash different scores into the same value — 0.412 and 0.418 both become 0.40. Now those two transactions are **tied**, and you have lost your opinion about which should go first. You threw away ordering you had.

So we did not test it. There was nothing to test.

### 2. We removed class weighting

Fraud is only 1.76% of the data. The standard fix is to tell the model *"treat every fraud row as if it were 55 rows"* so it takes them seriously.

That fix is for a different job — the job where you must draw a line and say **yes, fraud** or **no, not fraud**.

You never draw a line. You just order the list.

So the fix was not solving your problem, it was **distorting your list**. Removing it was the single biggest gain of the entire competition. Lesson 9 is about that.

### 3. We were free to mix models however we liked

Because the output numbers never have to mean anything, the team could blend the tree models and the neural network in whatever proportion scored best — 48/52, then 12% — without worrying whether the mixed numbers still made sense as probabilities.

They did not need to make sense. They only needed to sort correctly.

**A correction worth recording:** an earlier version of this lesson said the neural network is blended *by rank* rather than by probability. That claim came from the **repo's** copy of the champion notebook, whose header says "blended in at 12% by rank". The **submitted** champion does no such thing — the blend is a plain weighted average of probabilities. Work from the champion.

### 4. The tree counts were chosen against the real metric

The **champion does not early-stop at all** — no callbacks, no validation watching, no custom metric function. It trains a fixed 882 trees and 433 trees, hard-coded.

What is true: those two numbers were *discovered* by earlier experiments that did watch average precision, and then written into the final pipeline as constants. So the claim describes how the numbers were found, not what the submitted notebook does.

If a judge asks how you chose 882 trees, the honest answer is: *earlier experiments early-stopped on average precision — the competition metric itself, not a stand-in — and we fixed the resulting round counts for the final run so it is fully deterministic.*

## Why this metric is hard

You are finding roughly **4,600 frauds hidden among 262,648 transactions.**

The obvious ones — someone spends 300,000 taka at 3 AM — are easy. Everyone catches those. They do not decide the competition.

Your score is decided by the **hard middle**: fraud that looks perfectly normal. Like device D000775 — twelve transactions between 340 and 780 taka, at a restaurant, in the evening, on a mobile app. Nothing about any single one looks wrong.

Getting *those* near the top of the list is the whole game, and it is why the scores look low. 0.565 out of 1.0 sounds mediocre until you remember random guessing scores **0.0176**.

## Why it is the right metric anyway

Because it matches the real job.

A fraud team can review maybe 200 alerts a day. They do not need a model that is "accurate" — they need **the top of the list to be full of real fraud.**

That is precisely what this metric measures.

Good closing line if a judge asks why this metric: *it measures the only thing the business actually cares about — whether the top of the queue is worth working.*

## Say it like this

> We give every transaction a suspicion score, and those scores get sorted into a queue. The grade is: how much real fraud is near the front. Only the order counts — the numbers themselves are discarded. That is why we removed class weighting and skipped calibration, and why our tree counts were chosen against average precision itself rather than a stand-in measure. Random guessing scores 0.0176. We scored 0.56548.

# Lesson 8 — How the model actually produces the ranking

Think of it as **five people looking at one transaction, then agreeing on how worried to be.**

## Stage 1 — Translate the transaction into something meaningful

The raw transaction says: *672 taka, fuel merchant, mobile app, 11 PM.*

That is useless on its own. Millions of those happen.

So first we rewrite it as a description that actually means something:

> *This is 64 times what this customer normally spends. It is on a device that has done 12 transactions in the last five minutes. That device sits in a cluster of 340 linked accounts. And this customer has never once transacted at this hour.*

Same event. Completely different picture. That rewriting is the 160 features.

## Stage 2 — How a tree model thinks

The easiest way to understand gradient boosting.

Imagine you are guessing someone's weight.

- **Assistant 1** guesses: *70 kg.* He is off by 8.
- **Assistant 2** does not guess the weight. He only guesses **how wrong assistant 1 was**: *"add 5."*
- **Assistant 3** guesses what is *still* wrong: *"add 2."*
- **Assistant 4:** *"take off 0.5."*

Add them all up and you are very close. **Nobody solved the problem. Each one only cleaned up the leftover mistake of everyone before them.**

That is a gradient-boosted model. Each "assistant" is a small decision tree — a short list of yes/no questions:

> *Is the amount more than 8x this customer's normal?* -> yes
> *Has this device done more than 3 transactions this hour?* -> yes
> -> *nudge suspicion up a little*

One tree is nearly worthless. **882 of them, each fixing what the last ones got wrong, is a strong model.**

## Stage 3 — Why there are two of them

You have two advisors.

**Advisor A** has read **all six and a half months** of case files. Very experienced. But most of what she has read is *old* fraud — the kind that stopped working in July.

**Advisor B** has only read the **last 90 days**. Much less experience. But everything she knows is current.

Neither is right alone. Experience matters, but so does being up to date.

So you take both opinions:

> **48% Advisor A, 52% Advisor B**

**Advisor B gets the bigger vote** — despite having read far less. That is a deliberate statement the team made: *the test period is entirely after fraud changed shape, so being current beats being experienced.*

A good thing to be able to say. It shows the number was reasoned, not guessed.

## Stage 4 — The third opinion, worth 12%

There is a third advisor: the neural network.

On her own, she is **worse** than the other two. Clearly worse — 0.5173 alone.

But she is wrong about *different transactions*. The tree advisors think in hard cutoffs — "above 8, below 8." She thinks in softer, blurrier patterns. So on some transactions where both trees are confused, she is not.

You do not put her in charge. You give her a **small vote — 12%** — just enough to nudge the final answer where the others are shaky.

12% is not a round number someone liked. It is where testing showed the benefit peaked. More than that and you start importing her weakness; less and you gain nothing.

**Note:** this blend is a plain weighted average of the two probabilities. The repo's copy of the notebook describes it as a rank blend; the submitted champion does not do that.

## Stage 5 — Let neighbours speak for each other

The last step, and the one that won the score.

So far every transaction has been judged **completely alone**. But fraud does not happen alone. It happens in bursts.

Picture ten transactions on one phone in half an hour. Individually, each looks *mildly* odd — not enough to flag. The model looks at each one separately, shrugs ten times, and misses all ten.

So the last step is: **every transaction gets half its own score, and half the average score of its neighbours** — other transactions on the same customer or device within thirty minutes.

Now those ten mildly-odd transactions all pull each other upward. Together they become obviously suspicious. Apart, none of them were.

**One important rule:** a transaction is not included in its own neighbour average. **It cannot vouch for itself.**

And if a transaction genuinely has no neighbours — nothing else on that device nearby in time — nothing happens to it at all. That is why **95.3% of transactions pass through completely untouched.** This step does not create the ranking. It cleans up the edges of it.

## Then: sort

262,648 final numbers. Sort them highest to lowest.

**That order is your answer.** That is the whole submission.

## Two details worth knowing

**The average score is 0.01459** — almost exactly the real fraud rate of 1.76%. The model is not wildly over- or under-suspicious. Most transactions get a tiny number, a small tail gets high ones.

**Before saving, three things are checked:** no blanks, every score between 0 and 1, and the rows in exactly the order the organisers expect.

That last check matters more than it sounds. Submit perfect scores attached to the wrong rows and you score roughly zero — **and nothing warns you.** It just looks like your model was terrible.

## The one-paragraph version

> 160 behavioural features go into two gradient-boosted models — one trained on all history, one on the last 90 days — blended 48/52. A small neural network is mixed in at 12% because it fails on different rows than the trees do. Finally each score is averaged with its entity's neighbours within thirty minutes, so transactions that cluster can lift each other. Sort the results, and the order is the answer.

|---|---|---|

| Neural network blend | "12% by rank" | 12% by plain probability average |

Same cause: the `final_submission/` copy of the champion notebook was written *about* the pipeline rather than *from* it. That is the copy sitting in the reproducibility folder, so a reviewer who reads its header and then reads its code will find the mismatches.

# Lesson 9 — The biggest win was a deletion

Verified in the champion: the words `scale_pos_weight` **do not appear anywhere in it.** That absence is the single largest gain of the competition.

*(The measurements below come from the team's experiment log, not from the champion notebook. What the champion demonstrates is the decision — the parameter is gone.)*

## The problem everyone thinks they have

Only **1.76%** of the transactions are fraud. Roughly 1 in 57.

Every textbook, tutorial and blog post calls this "class imbalance" and warns that models struggle with it. The standard fix has a name and a knob, and the team turned that knob on **day one, before writing any features.**

The knob says: *treat every fraud row as if it were 55 rows.*

Why 55? Because there are about 55 legitimate transactions for every fraudulent one. So you weight fraud 55x to make the model take it as seriously as the legitimate majority.

This is completely standard advice. It is also, for this competition, **wrong.**

## Why it is wrong here

The fix solves a real problem — but a *different* problem from yours.

**Think of a doctor in an emergency room.**

You tell them: *"missing a heart attack is 55 times worse than a false alarm."* Sensible instruction. The doctor becomes cautious, sends more people for scans, and misses fewer heart attacks. Exactly what you wanted.

But now change the job. The doctor is not deciding who gets scanned. **The doctor is writing an ordered waiting list**, and will be graded on whether the sickest patients are near the top.

Under that same instruction — *"chest pain is 55x more important"* — the doctor stops making fine distinctions. Everyone with any chest symptom gets pushed toward the top as a block. The careful ordering *within* that group, which is exactly what is being graded, gets lost.

**That is your situation.** The weighting told the model to take fraud seriously. But you never make a yes/no call. You only produce an ordered list. The instruction made the model coarser precisely where you needed it sharpest.

## What the measurements showed

The team eventually turned the knob down step by step, on a July validation window:

| Fraud rows weighted as... | Score |
|---|---|
| 55 rows (the inherited setting) | 0.5045 |
| 10 rows | 0.5069 |
| 7.4 rows | 0.5091 |
| **1 row — no weighting at all** | **0.5116** |

**Every single step down improved the score.** No peak in the middle, no optimum to find. The best setting was to remove it entirely.

That pattern matters. A result that improves monotonically all the way to zero is not a lucky measurement — it is a mechanism. The knob was never helping; it was only ever costing.

**On the leaderboard it was worth +0.026** — more than every feature engineered that day combined, and the largest single gain of the competition.

## And here is the part worth presenting

This is the story judges will remember, and it is not flattering. Say it anyway.

The setting was written into the team's plan **in round one**, before any modelling. It looked obviously correct — it is what everyone does.

Then came days of hyperparameter tuning. Learning rates, tree sizes, regularisation, sampling rates. Dozens of experiments.

**Every one of those experiments held the weighting fixed at 55.**

Nobody varied it, because nobody thought of it as a choice. It was in the "already decided" pile. So it sat there, quietly costing more than anything else in the pipeline, through the majority of a 40-hour competition.

When it finally came out, something uncomfortable followed: **every conclusion drawn while it was on became void.** All that tuning had found the best settings *for a distorted model*. The whole hyperparameter search had to be run again from scratch.

## The lesson, in the form to say out loud

> Our biggest gain came from deleting a line we had never questioned — and it cost us more than any feature we added. The parameter had been in our plan since round one, and every sweep we ran held it fixed, so it went untested through most of the competition. When we removed it, every conclusion we had drawn under it was void and we had to re-tune from scratch.

Three reasons that lands well:

**It shows you understand your metric.** You can explain *why* it hurt — a ranking task versus a decision task — not just that it did.

**It is evidence, not opinion.** The monotonic table is four measurements pointing the same direction.

**It is honest about a mistake.** A team saying "our biggest win was fixing our own unexamined assumption" reads as far more trustworthy than a team with only successes. The competition guidance says it directly: *rigor is rewarded in judging.*

## The general principle underneath

The dangerous settings are not the ones you are unsure about — you test those.

They are the ones you are *certain* about. Certainty is what stops you from testing.

The team had two of these. This was one. The other was drawing conclusions from experiments too small to be real, which is Lesson 10.

# Sidebar to Lesson 9 — Ranking vs weighting: not a choice we made

A confusion worth clearing up, because the phrasing "ranking instead of weighting" makes them sound like two options we picked between. They are not.

## Ranking was given to us

**We did not choose ranking.** The organisers chose it. The competition's metric sorts your transactions and grades the order. That is fixed, decided before you started, and you cannot opt out. **Ranking is the task.**

**Weighting was a setting inside our model** — one knob among many, which we had switched on.

So the question was never *"should we rank or should we weight?"* It was:

> *Does this knob help us produce a better ordering?*

The answer was no, so we turned it off. Same task before and after — we just stopped doing something that was hurting.

## Why the knob hurt the ordering

**The model does not know which transactions are fraud.** It learns rules from patterns — *"if the amount is 60x normal and the device is busy, be suspicious."*

Now you tell it: **"every fraud you miss costs 55 times more than a false alarm."**

The model does exactly as instructed. It becomes generous. It widens its rules so almost nothing fraudulent slips past:

> *"Anything even slightly unusual — push it up."*

And it works. It does catch the frauds. **But it drags a huge number of innocent transactions up with them.**

Now look at the list. The top is crowded with ordinary transactions that merely *resembled* fraud. The real frauds are up there too — buried among false alarms.

**And "how clean is the top of the list" is literally the score.**

Turn the knob off and the model has no reason to be generous. It only pushes up what it is genuinely confident about. Fewer innocent transactions sneak into the top. **The list gets cleaner exactly where you are graded.**

## The same thing as a picture

With weighting at 55x — model is nervous, flags generously:

```
top of list ->  fraud, normal, normal, fraud, normal, normal, normal, fraud ...
```

Without weighting — model is picky, only commits when sure:

```
top of list ->  fraud, fraud, normal, fraud, normal, fraud, normal, normal ...
```

Same model, same data, same frauds in the file. The second list finds them faster, and that is the entire grade.

## Why the advice exists at all

Because usually it is right.

If your job is to make a **decision** — block this card, freeze that account — then missing a fraud really does cost far more than an unnecessary phone call. Weighting is the correct tool for that job, which is why it is in every textbook.

The team applied a good tool to the wrong job. The tool assumes you are deciding. We were sorting.

## The one-line version

> We never chose ranking — the competition's metric does the ranking, and that was fixed. Class weighting was a setting we had switched on, and it made the model flag too generously, which crowded the top of our list with false alarms. Removing it made the same ranking cleaner, and that was worth +0.026.

# Lesson 10 — The day we found out we had been measuring nothing

This is the least glamorous part of the project and the most impressive.

## Where the randomness comes from

The model does not build the same way twice unless you force it to.

Two settings in the champion cause this:

**It only looks at 85% of the transactions** when building each tree, chosen at random. **And only 85% of the features**, also chosen at random.

Why deliberately throw away 15%? Because a model that sees everything every time memorises quirks in the training data. Showing it random subsets forces it to find patterns that hold generally rather than accidents that happen to sit in the data.

It works. But it means **the model is slightly different every time you build it** — different random subsets, different trees, slightly different scores.

## Why this quietly ruins experiments

Imagine your bathroom scale wobbles by about half a kilo. Step on, 70.2. Step off, step on again, 69.8.

Now you try a new diet for a week. You weigh in: **you are down 0.3 kg.**

Did the diet work?

**You have no idea.** 0.3 is smaller than your scale's wobble. You would have seen that number whether you dieted or not.

Now imagine you spend a week testing seven different diets that way, keeping the ones that "worked" and abandoning the ones that "did not."

**You have learned nothing. You have recorded seven coin flips and called it research.**

## That is exactly what happened

The team ran seven experiments in a row:

- CatBoost, a different model type
- The first hyperparameter sweep
- Recency weighting
- Graph features
- Stationarity fixes
- Ratio features
- Expanded time windows

Every single one produced a change of **0.0015 to 0.002**. Some looked like small wins, some like small losses. Decisions were made off them. The project moved forward on them.

Then someone did the thing nobody had done: **ran the same model twice, changing nothing but the random seed.**

The score moved by **0.0020**.

**Every one of those seven results had been inside the wobble of the instrument.** None of them meant anything. Hours of work had produced seven readings of the scale, not seven findings.

## The rule that came out of it

From that point on, nothing counted unless:

1. **It was measured across several random seeds and averaged** — one reading is not a measurement, and
2. **The effect was clearly bigger than 0.0020** — comfortably above the wobble, not just past it.

The team went further and set the bar at **+0.004** — twice the noise floor — before an idea could be adopted.

## What that rule then killed

Ideas that looked like they worked, and were rejected anyway:

| Idea | Measured | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Target encoding | +0.0031 | below the bar — rejected |
| A learned stacking layer | +0.0029 | below the bar — rejected |
| Self-training | +0.0023 | below the bar — rejected |
| Drift normalisation | +0.0001 | pure noise — rejected |

Every one of those is a **positive number**. Every one was thrown away.

That is the discipline. It is easy to reject something that scored negative. Rejecting things that appear to help — because they do not appear to help *enough to be sure* — is the hard part, and it is what stops you shipping a pile of imaginary improvements.

## What the champion does about randomness

Two things, and it is worth being precise since it is what a reviewer will check.

**It pins every source of randomness.** The champion fixes the seed for the tree sampling, the feature sampling, the neural network's initialisation, and the general random number generator — all to 42. Run it twice on the same machine, get the same answer.

**It does not average across seeds.** It trains exactly two tree models and one neural network, single seed each. That is a deliberate trade: seed averaging costs several times the compute, and the notebook is built to run end to end in about fifteen minutes.

## And this explains the reproduction gap

The champion notebook's header claims a local score of **0.5359**, but the recorded run of that same notebook printed **0.5341**.

The difference is **0.0018.**

**That is inside the 0.0020 noise floor the team measured.**

So it is not a bug and not a broken pipeline. It is a single-seed model rebuilt on different hardware — the neural network in particular does not reproduce bit-for-bit across processors — landing exactly where the team's own measurement says it should land.

A strong answer, and it uses your own rigor to explain your own imperfection:

> Our notebook reproduces the method exactly and the score to within our measured noise floor of 0.0020. The 0.0018 gap between the header and the recorded run is single-seed variation on different hardware — we measured that floor early in the competition and it governs how we read every result, including this one.

## Why present this at all

Because it is the part of the work that generalises beyond a datathon, and judges know it.

Anybody can add features. Very few teams stop and ask *"can my measuring instrument even see the effect I think I am seeing?"* — and most of those who do not ask spend their competition confidently building on findings that were never there.

> **Our biggest methodological change was not a technique. It was discovering that seven of our experiments had been inside our own noise floor, and rebuilding how we measured everything after that.**
